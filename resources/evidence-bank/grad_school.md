Bribri Indigenous Rights on the Ground 

Bryan Johns 

In September of 2007, the United Nations General Assembly adopted the Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples.  While not legally binding, the Declaration does represent a step forward in the evolution of international law safeguarding the rights of these historically disadvantaged minorities, setting the tone and direction for the future development of international law, as ‘soft’ law moves towards hard law.  While in many ways similar to other international human rights instruments, the Declaration differs in specifically enumerating the rights of indigenous peoples as a group, with special provisions for the preservation of their diverse cultures, including languages, handicrafts, technologies, livelihoods, customs, religions, etc.  These collective community rights, as opposed to traditional, individual-focused human rights, are a major reason for a separate instrument to protect indigenous rights.  The Declaration also specifically spelled out the right to self-determination and the ‘right to development’1, so that indigenous groups will always be consulted over matters that concern them, and ensuring that they can pursue their own vision of economic and social development.  Overall, when compared with these rights, the Bribri seem to be doing fairly well, given the limitations of their situation and despite the benign neglect of the national government of Costa Rica. 

Right to culture/identity 

The Bribri have preserved their independent cultural identity2, although that culture is obviously in a state of flux and change as it encounters the outside world.  We saw video footage of a mass community action to move one family’s ancestral stone from their former home to the new one.  Several of us participated in the traditional King Vulture dance.  We visited a Bribri cultural center, built to assist the local spiritual leader, the Agua, in preserving the lore of the community – ancient dances, chants, ceremonies, oral histories, traditional herbal medicines, and the like.  The casa conica is a beautiful symbol of the strength of their cultural identity.  We all purchased some of the traditional Bribri crafts.  They still practice traditional forms of preparing rice and cacao, and creating canoes and palm roofs.  These traditions are very much alive and well, and do not seem to be under any direct threat. 

However, the creeping influence of outsiders can clearly be seen, undermining the strength of these traditions.  I remember a group of young Bribri boys breakdancing to reggae one night.  I loved seeing a nun ride by on an ATV, both of which are obviously imports.  Midwives are disappearing as the Bribri adopt ‘modern’ birthing practices.  This is not to pass judgment, dubbing any of this ‘bad’, or mourning the ‘good old days’ before Western influence.  Although the nun (and Catholicism) may be an import, the German Catholics have apparently funded a number of development projects around Amubri, such as the local radio station and the plantain/banana chip factory.  While forcing any indigenous culture to assimilate into the dominant host culture is morally repugnant, not to mention forbidden3, it is a fact that cultures inevitably change.  The right of the Bribri to adapt and alter their culture must be respected.  I wish I knew the proportion of the population subscribing to Christianity versus the traditional Bribri animist religion, one concrete indicator of cultural change over time. 

The oft-stressed right to language is a fascinating example of how the Bribri culture is shifting.  More than just a form of communication, embodied within any language is a whole system of values, ideas, and symbols: an entire worldview.  Many indigenous languages are dying throughout the world, potently symbolic of the loss of the parent cultures.  Like their overall culture, the Bribri are not in immediate danger of losing their language.  A mere twenty years ago, some graduate students developed a written form of the language.  Books have been written and published in Bribri.  A Costa Rican university is preparing a Bribri-Spanish dictionary.  These are positive signs for the preservation of the language.  On the other hand, the local public school lessons are taught in Spanish instead of Bribri, despite the Declaration4.  The school holds exactly the same number of weekly Bribri language classes as English classes, which, based upon my interaction with local kids speaking English, is far from sufficient to give these children anything close to fluency in Bribri.  It seems as though the Costa Rican Ministry of Education only makes a half-hearted attempt to fulfill the obligation to respect indigenous right to language, something slightly better than benign indifference.  I don’t know how much Bribri is used in home or community life, so I worry about the long-term survival of the language. 

The right to self-determination and development 

The Declaration detailed the indigenous right to self-determination, the first time such an unqualified right has been explicitly stated for a sub-national group as an international norm5.  This right appears to be respected, although that probably is the result of neglect rather than anything else.  The Bribri appear to have a healthy degree of autonomy over their own affairs, managing such domestic affairs as divorce proceedings.  There is a form of indigenous customary law, but I unfortunately didn’t learn many specifics.  The only specific example that I heard of was an example of the failure of indigenous governance.  For legal enforcement, local regulations banning certain chemicals from use in the cultivation of bananas depend upon a system of denouncement, wherein anybody can anonymously report a member of the community for the use of prohibited chemicals.  The system doesn’t work, as the members of this tiny community refuse to report their neighbors; they doubt that their anonymity will be maintained.  Social cohesion and peace are valued over the enforcement of the law.  This leads to the situation where anyone walking down the street can see the glaringly obvious evidence of violation of the law: the blue plastic bags hanging over banana bunches.  Everyone knows the lawbreakers, and nothing is done.  I wish I knew more about the local system of governance: laws, punishments, enforcement (or lack thereof), governing bodies, and the proceedings of the judicial system.  Without further information, it is hard to make any observations on the effectiveness of Bribri self-rule, or how it interacts with the Costa Rican government, much less international law. (In theory they can use the C.R. state legal system to denounce that and other kinds of contamination. They have resorted in the past to national constitutional court, to stop, for instance, exploitation of the river materials, gravel, by external people, of course much need to be done to deal with banana plantation owner, maybe  employer to some of them) 

I suspect the Costa Rican government mostly ignores the Bribri, as I know of no specific legislation supporting indigenous groups, other than the 1977 law establishing self-governing indigenous reserves around the nation.  If valuable minerals were found on Bribri territory, like with the aboriginals of northern Australia in the video we watched, the situation might be different.  Ominously, with rumors of gold in the Talamanca hills, there have been helicopter mineral exploration fly-overs.  There is a local, 12-member council on development, supposedly informed of their rights under national and international law.  Membership should to rotate on a yearly basis, although I heard conflicting reports on whether or not that actually happens.  This Council on Development has been resisting these mining explorations, although I don’t know specifics.  It didn’t sound like protest, so it was presumably done through legal or government channels.  This is the only reference I’ve heard of akin to the right to consultation or the right to free, prior, informed consent6.  It is also the only direct reference I’m privy to regarding the interaction between the Bribri and the Costa Rican government. (There is also the Municipality in Bribri, who is governmental and coordinates with the bribri association, another way of State interaction is through the Judicial, Constitutional court, which in various instances has protected right to consultation and other rights included in 169 ILO Treaty) To be fair, having traveled around Costa Rica for months, I’d guess that this neglect has less to do with their indigenous identity and more to do with the neglect of remote rural areas by the large urban population centers, a rural-urban pattern of neglect I’ve noticed across the globe.  On the dubiously positive side, this means the Bribri are not being singled out because of their indigenous character; they and the rest of the rural poor are being equally ignored (discriminated against?) by urban indifference.  It is worth mentioning that there has apparently never been an indigenous member of the Costa Rican legislature.  Costa Rica appears to do a good job of respecting (not violating) indigenous rights, but seems to lack the specific legislative measures and ‘rights to redress’ to ensure and fulfill these rights7.  (well said) 

We heard few reports of direct Costa Rican government interference in Bribri affairs.  Occasional police raids destroying marijuana plantations in the hills surprised me, as many Native American communities in the United States can legally cultivate marijuana in their sovereign territory.  This form of government interference has impacted the right to health care and cleanliness of the environment, since some of these raids involved the aerial dumping of herbicides, which then leach out into the surrounding environment.  More helpfully, the police have also supported women’s rights by coming into the community to arrest domestic abuse offenders.  We did hear of one specific example of the Costa Rican judicial system going beyond indifference to actively supporting indigenous rights, specifically the right to territory8.  Reminiscent of the Endorois case9, the Keköldi were somehow deemed to be living on land owned by an Italian and were forcibly evicted.  They were restored to their traditional territory, and will receive reparations. 

The right to self-determination includes economic development10 over which the Bribri have much independence, although that independence is hollow unless coupled with choices for livelihood options.  In Amubri, all we saw for employment options were a few stores and restaurants, plus minimal tourism.  Agriculture is the major livelihood option for the Bribri, but that comes with ecological and marketing limitations.  Ecologically, the Bribri can only grow certain types of crops, with bananas, plantains, cacao, and guayaba being the main commercial crops.  Associacion de Productos Pequeños de Talamanca (APPTA) has a monopoly over the purchase of the banana and cacao crops.  While seemingly benign, any monopoly troubles me.  With plantains and guayaba, there are slightly more options, as several middle-man purchasers wait across the river, competing with each other and providing better prices to the producers.  These agricultural products are the main leverage that the community has to purchase anything that cannot be produced locally.  The plantain chip factory appears to be the only innovative ‘value-adding’ productive activity.  The Bribri economic freedom borders on meaningless, considering the few options open to them.  The surprise is how much progress they have made towards diversifying their economy and improving their community within these given constraints. 

A laudable example of success, the rural tourism center at Yorkin, established twenty years ago by the women’s cooperative, Estibrawpa, exemplifies how far an indigenous community can implement their own right to development.  Via their cooperative, they managed to spread the idea of gender equality.  By removing the men from their seasonal employment in the chemical-laden, disease- and sterilization-inducing banana fields, they helped improve the health of the community.  They claim to have shifted their dominant economic income away from agriculture, such that 70% of their income comes from the tourism project, with the remaining 30% from agriculture.  They maintain their cultural traditions, incorporating them into the tourism project, offering demonstrations of rice and cacao preparation, culinary classes, ‘technologies’ like palm roof making, art & handicraft lessons, Bribri language courses, walks with medicinal plant lectures, as well as horseback riding and the opportunity to use a bow & arrow.  They rotate job assignments throughout every family to ensure the entire community profits.  This success story is the perfect counter-point to cynicism over the current state of the Bribri. 

Odds & Ends 

On a number of broad social indicators11, the Bribri seem to be improving.  Notions of gender equality12 seem to have percolated through the community, with the women’s cooperatives of Yorkin and Amubri taking strong community leadership roles, developing economic activities, spreading organic agricultural practices, and providing classes on computers and entrepreneurship.  Presumably, this shift towards gender equality has spread into family life, although there are still cases of domestic abuse.  As I didn’t see any glaring cases of malnutrition or other health deficiencies13, the overall health of the community seems to be decent, considering their distance from (hence, access to) formal health care facilities – the only ambulance I saw was across the river in Suretka.  The shift away towards organic agriculture (and banana cultivation) has had positive health impacts.  I have no idea how well the Costa Rican system of social security works (or doesn’t) here.  Unfortunately, without hard data on changes in indicators like life expectancy over time, I cannot say for sure if my impression is accurate.  Presumably, the traditional Bribri education system is gone, replaced by government schools, to which the Bribri have full access.  I have the impression that the Bribri as a whole are becoming more educated, but I again lack the necessary data14. 

Environmentally, the Bribri formally have control over their natural resources15, but have delivered mixed results while managing them.  The failure of local regulation over the usage of chemicals in banana cultivation is a good example.  Another obvious example was the conspicuous absence of monkeys, sloths, and numerous species of birds, all hunted to local extinction.  I visited the iguana nursery at Keköldi, established to repopulate the Caribbean coast species, endangered by over-exploitation.  Rather than being irritated by outside interference (or the lack of ‘consultation’), I heard the opinion voiced that the Bribri are lucky to have MINAET ban the Bribri from hunting.  The introduction of organic farming techniques and the iguana nursery, along with the conservation signs posted throughout Yorkin, are all positive examples of a growing environmental consciousness, but I still fear for the environment under Bribri management.  (Bribri´s hunting was due to hunger and limited resources, for instance to have or buy chickens, instead of eating everything Nature had then, so clearly options for livelihoods were needed, some of which are provided with the new projects, by NGO and universities, though.  Also their increased awareness of eco tourism as a source of income).  As mentioned before in the context of the right to self-determination and mineral exploration, the indigenous right to control over natural resources stands a high chance of coming into conflict with national (and business) interests, a key challenge for the future of international law.  (Very true) 

Conclusion 

Major issues remain to be resolved in international law.  For example, what happens when the national interest conflicts with the indigenous right to development and free, prior, informed consent16?  The Boruca face a big dam project right now, which presumably could end up in the hands of the Inter-American Court of Human Rights, which historically has a tendency to allow the State some freedom in expropriating land and resources to the detriment of indigenous groups17.  Another tension lies in the relationship between human rights and collective rights, and how to choose which supersedes the other, and when; for instance, when a punishment meted out under customary law, such as banishment, which can be construed as torture and would therefore conflict with human rights norms18.  How can two separate legal codes (indigenous and national) exist in the same place?  How will the right to development and control over natural resources play out when faced with eminent domain and the national good, or when confronted with the vested interests of the powerful, as in the case of the Australian and Amazonian videos we watched, especially since the Declaration isn’t even legally binding? 

Despite these lingering large-scale issues, in Bribri, the overarching theme that I see is one of slow, incremental progress, not unlike the 25-year struggle to draft the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples.  The Bribri seem to face no direct threat to their rights.  They have a considerable degree of autonomy, and seem to have a history of gradually exercising and expanding their rights.  Health and education levels have gone up, gender equality has spread, economic activity has expanded, environmental exploitation has gone down, and the indigenous have some respect under the Costa Rican government.  Cultural traditions are very much alive, although morphing in response to people’s changing lives and outside influence.  However, the Costa Rican government plays a very passive role in nurturing indigenous rights, with little proactive action.  The Declaration seems to have very little to do with actions on the ground; there appears to be a huge disconnect between the international and grassroots levels.  The Bribri have achieved much in this environment of benign neglect.  Internationally recognized indigenous rights need to be reinforced by national law to eliminate potential threats that could still affect the Bribri.

Conservation & Development

Course Description


This course explores the past, present, and future of efforts to address poverty and environmental degradation throughout the world. Although in the past these issues were often treated separately—indeed, in many cases they were seen as diametrically opposed—today it is increasingly argued that they are intricately intertwined in myriad ways and thus must be addressed simultaneously. We’ll begin by examining the history of international development and conservation interventions, respectively, analyzing the ideology or “discourse” informing their practice, and discussing their contemporary convergence under the rubric of “sustainable development.” Then we’ll explore various practical and conceptual issues involved in pursuing ecological, economic, and social sustainability. We’ll finish by reviewing contemporary directions and controversies in the field and brainstorming new possibilities for the future.

Reflecting both the newfound recognition within policy circles of the importance of the complex human dynamics involved in addressing poverty and environmental degradation and the relative neglect of such considerations in the past, this class will emphasize the social scientific study of conservation/

development practice, exploring the political, economic, social, cultural, and logistical issues involved in implementing successful measures. Rather than focusing on specific themes or topics within conservation/development (i.e., forestry, ecotourism, agriculture, etc.), we’ll emphasize the core conceptual issues that crosscut and underlie all specific foci. We’ll try to push our understanding of these issues as deep as possible, probing their philosophical roots and cultural consequences. In this effort, we’ll also reflect critically of our own beliefs, values, and assumptions in order to develop sensitivity to the types of cultural differences likely to influence interventions’ success in diverse parts of the world.

Course Orientation/Objectives


While this course is part of a professional master’s program, its approach will be more conceptual/theoretical than skills-based. The knowledge and perspective gained from this course, however, will be directly applicable to students’ professional praxis. Policy Analysis for Global Environmental Politics

COURSE DESCRIPTION
This course offers an advanced treatment of methods of environmental policy analysis.
The course is intended to serve graduate students in GEP, NRSD and other programs by
providing “critical literacy” in some of the analytic techniques most widely used in the making,
implementation, and evaluation of environmental policies. “Critical literacy” in this context means
understanding not only the methods and applications of policy-analytic techniques, but also their
limitations, the legal and bureaucratic/administrative contexts within which they are used, and
the ongoing controversies that surround their use.
Specific techniques and topics to be studied include basic data analysis and trend
assessment, risk assessment, comparative risk assessment, cost-benefit analysis,
environmental impact assessment, rapid appraisal, participatory and community-based
techniques of data collection and evaluation, sustainability indicators, social marketing,
rulemaking notice and comment processes, and evaluating policy effectiveness. Given the
emphasis on understanding these techniques in practice, the reading for this class will be
public documents containing policy analysis rather than traditional textbooks or
scholarly articles. Each week we will work through a case study in which a particular mode of
policy analysis played a part in shaping that case’s decisions, actions, and outcomes.
This is an advanced course that assumes substantial background in environmental
policy. It is strongly suggested that students in the class have taken SIS 660 (Environment and
Politics) or have a comparable background. I assume basic knowledge of that course’s material,
including the major issue areas that fall under the environmental rubric (air quality, water quality,
toxics, energy use, land use, climate change, forestry, natural resource management, and so
on); familiarity with core elements of the global environmental policy process (the development
regime, the trading system, international treaty negotiations, intergovernmental organizations);
and familiarity with some of the major controversies that crop up repeatedly in environmental

Subject: US EPA’s Toxic Release Inventory (TRI) 

 

Per the request of the Minister of the Environment, here’s a brief summary of the EPA’s TRI for consideration in our own efforts to establish a similar program.  Established in response to public demand for information about toxic chemical releases, the program collects self-reported data from facilities meeting specific reporting criteria.  It also collects data on the transfer between facilities and ultimate fate of all listed chemicals used in production to identify trends in pollution prevention, like reduced usage and recycling. 

 

The TRI program has been relatively successful, especially in providing information, although it does little to interpret the data.  The TRI program has expanded the access that local communities have to information on waste management and toxic releases, as the original law intended, and it has been accompanied by a reduction in the emission of toxins, the intent of an early addition to the law, although it is important to note that correlation does not prove causation.  While that reduction may be due to public pressure in response to TRI-data, it could be due to factors like changes in economic conditions or production methods.  As for its usefulness in enabling “informed decision making”, the pure data isn’t especially layperson-friendly.  The internet database is dense and difficult to navigate.  Once found, the data must be accompanied by further research; the website contains no information on toxicity of the listed chemicals, or the pathway that releases take in the environment.  Releases are not exposures, nor necessarily risks to public health. 

 

The TRI data is self-reported, often based on estimates, and is therefore only reliable for attaining a rough, order-of-magnitude picture, nothing more refined.  It is a broad, blunt, depiction of toxic releases. 

 

As a measure of toxic releases, the three largest sources of error in TRI data are: 

Critically, the data is self-reported, often relying solely on estimates rather than actual measurements.  This has led to sizeable ‘paper’ changes in reported data over the years as the estimation techniques at specific installations are revised.  Furthermore, the EPA provides little information on an independent verification process, suggesting little external monitoring and heightening the potential for grossly erroneous or outright falsified submissions of data. 

TRI reporting criteria exempts numerous installations, and therefore cannot provide a complete picture of toxic releases in a given locale.  Installations are not required to report if they operate outside of specific industrial sectors, if their chemical releases fall below minimum reporting thresholds, or if the installation employs fewer than ten people.  A concentration of such installations may lead to higher-than-expected toxins present in communities than the TRI data would state. 

TRI-data only covers certain chemicals, and again will not provide a complete picture of toxic releases.  The original list was a combination of information from the States of Maryland and New Jersey, with an appeals process to add or delete chemicals on the basis of research and political will. 

 

Computaphonavision’s request for regulatory freedom should be denied.  The real issue lies with the public health implications not of total flows, but for each specific chemical used, with attendant responsibilities to prevent harmful exposures.  Relying on a self-reported decline in toxic releases is unlikely to be politically viable with local communities, and may incentivize the company to hide any accidental large releases to avoid future regulation. 

 

In sum, the TRI program is a cost-effective way of gathering a crude, imprecise estimate of the final disposal of specifically identified toxic chemicals.

The Bank should provide reassurances that it will not recreate ‘power’ imbalances by taking this path, and clearly explain how increasing production will lead to reliable rural electrification. 

We advocate a diversified, decentralized micro‐generation approach to energy proliferation, focusing on decarbonized alternatives.  This approach benefits communities by sheltering them from volatile electric rates and enhancing local participation and control, democratizing energy production and enhancing energy security.  The Bank should coordinate with other agencies to provide technical training and microcredit loans to assist small and medium enterprises in renewable energy self‐sufficiency, leading to rural job creation directly in the energy sector and indirectly through economic growth.  The Bank should also address the inhibiting influence of intellectual property rights on the spread of clean energy technologies. 

The Bank’s Approach does not have an explicit plan for transitioning to a sustainable energy future.  The Bank must get out of reputation‐damaging extractive industry fossil fuels, especially coal, consistent with the precautionary principle.  The Bank should pressure borrower governments to shift fossil fuel subsidies and to renewables.  While the Bank should spread energy efficient technologies, the Approach must also promote reduced energy consumption through demand‐side management, such as through a polluter‐pays system where energy prices increase with consumption.  The Bank must adopt a system of greenhouse gas accounting in Bank investments, improving the Bank’s currently dubious credibility as a force for environmentally sustainable development.   The Bank should coordinate with climate debt funding from the historically responsible developed nations to avoid saddling developing nations with unnecessary debt.  These recommendations should be incorporated into a firmly targeted plan, with an accountability mechanism for failure to comply, for rapidly shifting the Bank away from the decaying fossil fuel energy paradigm and into a completely decarbonized energy portfolio. 

The Bank needs to set tangible commitments for creating long‐term change in the global energy mix while supporting the poor.  The draft Approach contains vague aspirations for an environmentally sustainable energy future, combined with poverty‐alleviating increased energy production; it lacks a specific causal chain explaining how the ends will be connected to the means.  Increasing centralized energy production and efficiency will not create the energy paradigm shift needed to avoid climate destruction.  To transition to an equitable, socially and ecologically just sustainable future, the world needs to adopt a decentralized, micro‐generation decarbonized energy supply, and the World Bank needs to lay out a specific plan for getting there yesterday.

The CLTS strategy effectively employs many of the McKenzie‐Mohr guidelines.  It stresses commitment, employs effective communication strategies, and pushes rapid social diffusion of new norms. The major failure is the threateningly negative focus.  All activities are meant to generate “an extreme level of disgust” (CLTS pg. 10).  By offering minimal assistance, CLTS risks inducing emotion‐focused coping (burying heads in the sand) if the community perceives it has little control. 

I feel unqualified to judge the effectiveness of intercultural applications of the McKenzie‐Mohr strategy; I haven’t studied multicultural social motivation, and McKenzie‐Mohr doesn’t address it.  It goes back to the old ‘nature vs. nurture’ debate: how much of the strategy targets evolutionary psychological hardwiring, and how much targets what is memetically – culturally – taught? A cop‐out argument is that the ‘Know Your Audience’ guideline (McKenzie‐Mohr pg. 41) conceivably covers all cultural variation, but the reality is I need more information to make a judgment.

INSUFFICIENT IMPACT ASSESSMENTS: 

The Phulbari Coal Mine 

 

Introduction 

The Summary Environmental Impact Assessment Report (SEIA) for the Phulbari Coal Mine Project in Bangladesh outlines the anticipated environmental impacts and mitigation measures for an ambitious category‐A (high risk) open‐pit coal extraction project.  Prepared by the Project developer, Asia Energy Corporation (Bangladesh) Pty Ltd, the document draws on some 110 studies to satisfy the Asian Development Bank’s due diligence requirement of the lending process.  Over nearly 40 years, the Project would mine possibly the largest coal reserve in the country, digging out a 5,192 ha patch of land (¶7) – almost the size of Manhattan – in the small northeastern Bangladeshi town of Phulbari, situated in one of the poorest regions of the country.  The resulting mine pit would force resettlement of 9,000 households, approximately 40,000 people, including part of the town of Phulbari itself (¶111). 

The Project plans to develop an ambitious transportation network to get the coal out of Phulbari.  The Project would upgrade 500 km of Bangladeshi railroad tracks to access domestic markets, connect to India, and reach international waterways for export.  To store coal and load it onto barges for export, the Project would build a new Coal Terminal in the city of Khulna on land owned by the local Port Authority, forcing the resettlement of 3,200 people (half characterized as squatters) that currently reside on the site (¶58).  Coal‐laden barges would chart a course down 107 km of waterways through the Sundarbans, the world’s largest mangrove forest, deemed an internationally significant wetland under the Ramsar Convention and home to the endangered Bengal tiger and Gangetic dolphin.  The barges would converge on a floating transfer site downstream, offloading the coal onto Panama Canal‐sized seagoing vessels that would return to the Indian Ocean down a 43 km channel explicitly dredged for the Project, squeezing between two Sundarban UNESCO World Heritage sites. 

Considering the potential size of the economic benefits and the sizeable environmental and social impacts, the proposed Project should be carefully scrutinized.  This SEIA reflects the reality of many SEIAs, wherein a potentially lucrative project is submitted by the developer for outside approval.  Critical literacy in ‘reading between the lines’ of a document with clear vested interests is a powerful step in guarding against potential abuses. 

Alternative Analysis 

The analysis of alternatives appears limited, promptly discarding the ‘No Project’ scenario as impractical (¶198‐200).  The SEIA claims the coal mine is necessary for Bangladesh’s economic future: depletion of natural gas reserves within 15 years will result in an increasing gap between electricity supply and demand, increased deforestation, and reduced air quality as biomass fuel fills the energy gap.  Phulbari coal is presented as the only alternative, providing additional benefits such as the diversification of energy supply, export earnings, employment opportunities, and local/regional economic and social development.  Domestic Phulbari coal is presented as the ONLY option for diversifying electric supply.  No mention is made of increasing the energy efficiency of natural gas, importing energy from India, encouraging renewable energy options, importing oil/diesel, or other possible international or domestic sources of coal.  While it’s unrealistic to expect this SEIA to explore the full range of options available to fill Bangladesh’s energy demands, the analysis of alternatives could be broader. 

The rest of the analysis of alternatives only explores technical variants on ways of getting the coal out of the ground and designing the transportation system (e.g. rail routes, size of the boat fleet).  Open‐pit mining wins over closed‐pit operations, due to safety concerns (underground and land subsidence), a poor coal recovery rate (less than 10%), and profitability.  The SEIA does not explore alternatives for exporting coal that do not ship through the Sundarbans.  Considering the sensitivity of the ecosystem, it seems reasonable to expect a rationale for choosing this river route (a sizeable, yet little‐trafficked, waterway), instead of shipping the coal through India, or using a waterway that circumvents the Sundarbans. 

Applicant‐Supplied Data 

The SEIA relies on information supplied by the applicant and lacks third‐party verification.  For example, Asia Energy supplies air quality measurements at the Phulbari mine and the Khulna facility based on particulate matter concentrations, with no mention of external monitoring or verification.  Their dispersion models predict that Project emissions will rarely exceed Bangladeshi standards, although the currently extant local and regional emissions already exceed limits (¶68‐69); the mine will exacerbate a dangerous situation, but it will mitigate emissions as much as possible with an automated sprinkler system for dust suppression.  The SEIA similarly devotes a substantial portion to detailing self‐supplied, unverified information on nuisance noise levels. 

The mine plans to lower the water table to the base of the mine pit.  The mining company utilized its own pumping test investigations to develop a projected model of the impact of dewatering activities.  They assert that dewatering will lower groundwater levels by 25 meters within a 4 km radius of the mine, and 15 meters within a 6 km radius, potentially resulting in decreased water availability for domestic and agricultural use, reduced water flow in rivers, land settlement, and lowered groundwater quality (¶93).  Groundwater aquifer injection is supposed to mitigate this.  The Project will supply reticulated groundwater from dewatering operations to the community for domestic and agricultural use, and monitor shallower tubewells to ensure a sufficient supply of water.  The Project predicts that groundwater withdrawal will be adequate for these purposes (¶100).  Their investigations “indicate that arsenic is not currently a problem in the Project area, and is unlikely to be released as a result of mine dewatering (¶94).”  No independent verification has been conducted. 

Consideration of the impact on biodiversity is extremely minimal.  “The proposed barging and shipping operations are considered unlikely to have any significant adverse impacts on river hydrology, sedimentation rates, or turbidity (¶144).”  There is no evidence to back up this claim.  The proposed vessels, with drafts of 12 meters, will displace a huge amount of water, and there is no indication that they have given thought to the impact on erosion in the surrounding islands.  The sundari trees are suffering in increasing numbers from the “top‐dying” phenomenon, resulting from unknown factors but theoretically linked to salinity changes, sediment deposition, and riverbank erosion (¶193).  As a vital core of the mangroves, fish nursery to the Bay of Bengal, these trees need extra care, and it would be nice to see Asia Energy’s plans beyond vague promises of monitoring for the future. 

Insufficient Information on Mitigation Measures 

The mitigation plan is barely covered in the document.  The document frequently makes reference to the Environmental Management Plan and the Construction Environmental Management Plan, both of which are not included in the SEIA.  For clues, one is forced to look through the attached 26 pages of tables of proposed mitigation measures (p. 55‐80), which only provides summarized one‐liners.  One would need to look in the supporting documents (10 volumes thereof) for the details.  While it is impossible to expect such an ambitious project to hold all of the relevant mitigation details, the document shunts a surprisingly large amount of information to the background details. 

There is a disturbing lack of information on the Sundarbans, surprising given the intense biodiversity of the region, which would lead one to expect plenty of reports from biologists.  “A detailed biodiversity study will be undertaken in relation to the proposed operations.  This will include a detailed review of existing information (including the references cited in this report), and detailed baseline surveys at representative aquatic, inter‐tidal, and shoreline sites along the proposed route (¶194i).”  The diversity and abundance of species is “most likely” higher downstream, although it should be noted that the population of the Gangetic dolphin has sharply declined due to water pollution and human encroachment, and the population of other species has also likely declined (¶35‐36).  They could and should conduct more rigorous studies before submitting the Project for approval. 

There does not appear to be a plan to mitigate the impact on the Sundarbans, other than to claim that “the Project will introduce stricter handling of wastewater and industrial waste in the area, so the water quality is likely to improve slowly (¶42).”  They promise to monitor soil bank erosion, suspended solid loads, dissolved oxygen levels, and to adjust the speed of vessels if necessary, or build engineering or vegetation bank stabilization works (p. 75‐76).  There are no steps beyond promising to develop a monitoring plan for biodiversity (p. 79).  “The processes of shoreline erosion and accretion within the SRF are highly complex… There are reports that net erosion is occurring along the sea face, although new islands are also emerging (¶39).”  They have no idea what’s going on with erosion; it seems quite likely that the introduction of larger, water‐displacing transport vessels will change erosion patterns.  While they may have established a number of rigorous mitigation measures for limiting the risk of coal and oil spillage, it seems as though they have a duty to do more, considering the biologically rich and endangered nature of the Sundarbans. 

The document admits that many of the proposed mitigation measures have yet to be devised.  “The EMPs have been developed without reference to detailed design… it is envisaged that the EMPs will be revised and updated following completion of the detailed design phase of the Project (¶230).”  The document focuses on the management framework over the EMP, assigning duties and accountability for implementation and monitoring, primarily to the Project environment department.  While it promises to inculcate “a culture of open communication and transparency in the workplace (¶241)” and mentions incident reporting and investigation, possibly resulting in corrective action reports, it is impossible to tell from the document how rigorously the Project will adhere to environmental and social standards.  The Project promises to have annual external auditing on an annual basis, submitted to senior mine management and the Board of Directors for review (¶248), with no mention of allowing access to outside authorities.  The SEIA mentions all best practices related to monitoring and evaluation, but it’s impossible to tell if it’s paying lip‐service, with environmental management a sidelined procedural nuisance of company culture, or if it’s a fully integrated component of the Project. 

The only risk assessment included in the SEIA covers the sensitive Sundarbans.  While acknowledging that a risk assessment necessitates evaluating potential consequences, likelihood of occurrence, and mitigation measures (¶255), it never addresses the potential severity of pollution from an oil spill in the Sundarbans, only addressing the likelihood and mitigation measures.  Vessels will use rapidly dissipating diesel fuel while in the river system to lower the environmental consequences of an oil spill.  The nature of the river will force boats to move at slower speeds, and the volume of vessel traffic throughout the river system is currently low (about one vessel per day), resulting in few opportunities for collision.  The sandy river floor reduces the risk of grounding, and contracting fleets with high safety ratings statistically greatly lowers the risk of accident.  It never addresses the possibility of increased boat traffic following the dredging of the river.  The Project intends to supply the area with modern specialized spill containment equipment and procedures.  It also never mentions the risk of fire hazards on boats.  While these measures are probably as much as can be expected, the SEIA should further address the potential consequences of a major oil spill in the sensitive Sudarbans. 

Social Impacts 

Despite frequently mentioning them in passing, the SEIA does not claim to summarize social impacts.  A tenacious search of the internet, however, failed to turn up the presumed companion piece, a Summary Social Impact Assessment.  The document briefly mentions the establishment of a resettlement plan, complete with compensation packages, livelihood restoration measures, and an indigenous people’s development plan.  It does not discuss the resettlement of squatters at the Khulna site, or the impact on fishermen in the Sundarbans.  There is no independent verification of the number of people to be resettled (40,000+), or for the assertion that “a significant reduction in land acquisition and population displacement is not possible without compromising the economic and technical viability of the project (¶112).”  Again, one must rely on the applicant’s assertion of the minimization of social impacts to the fullest extent possible; the unavailability of a companion social summary is quite troublesome. 

One of the interesting (arrogant) features of the mitigation plan is the suggestion to relocate schools in Khulna if operations are too loud (p. 72).  Considering the frequent pleas regarding the financial viability of the Project, it seems odd how often they promise to fund extra mitigation measures. 

The SEIA sketchily mentions community consultation.  Instead of referring to consent, “the entire process has been underpinned by free, prior, and informed consultations with stakeholders (¶280, emphasis added).”  The summary of information is vague, amounting to bulleted lists of stakeholders consulted and topics covered.  “Details of the public consultations undertaken during preparation of the environmental and social impact assessment studies are available in the supporting documents (¶237).”  The SEIA asserts these consultations have resulted in broad community support: “The Project, although opposed by some people, is supported by the majority of local community residents, even with the understanding that a considerable number of residents will have to be resettled (¶293).”  Despite the fact that the Project has triggered the ADB’s indigenous people’s safeguard, consultations only provided written and audiovisual information in Bengali and English, without any concession to indigenous languages (¶282).  It mentions a contract with one central NGO, curiously unidentified, with “details of the engagement of the NGOs [to] follow the detailed design phase (¶287).”  Detailed information about the consultation period would increase the reliability of the claim of broad community support, such as a summary of design changes incorporated based upon community and governmental input. 

Conclusion 

There is a natural tension between brevity and being informative; the document needs to be as short as possible to provide decisions makers with a rapid analysis, but it sacrifices necessary information as it becomes whittled down.  “In order to keep this SEIA as short and informative as possible in light of the vast quantity of documents: (i) this SEIA only goes into detail about important issues involving potentially large impacts to sensitive areas; and (ii) in cases where less impact is foreseen, this SEIA only briefly describes the likely environmental consequences (¶2).”  It’s a tension between readability and information, two different kinds of usefulness.  This document is a prime example of something whittled down too much.  While the target audience is ADB lenders, presumably highly knowledgeable about extractive industries and Bangladesh, the SEIA could do a better job of allaying suspicions for a potentially lucrative project in the heavily scrutinized mining sector.  Many important issues are negligibly mentioned, necessitating further digging within the supporting documents to find the answers, an extra step that acts as a barrier to further research. 

There are two ways to read this SEIA: charitably, as a good‐faith effort from a mining company utilizing all reasonable mitigation measures, or suspiciously, as an attempt to hide the true impact, a greedy wolf in poverty‐alleviating, environmentally‐friendly sheep’s clothing.  It comes down to a matter of trust, difficult for the tarnished reputation of the mining industry.  This SEIA comes across as long‐winded exercise in justification: decision‐making, rather than decision‐aiding, a sales pitch.  Impacts are minimized or framed positively, with railway vegetation depicted as a biological corridor and seedbank, the leftover mine hill in Phulbari as a tourist lookout in an otherwise flat landscape, the resettled employees of the local port authority in Khulna benefitting from reduced commute time to work, and the entire Project justified as a sustainable energy source essential to Bangladesh’s future economic development.  The document leaves grave questions unanswered.  Mitigation measures are vaguely specified, and much of the relevant data collection remains to be done.  The lack of easy access to a social impact assessment raises transparency concerns.  In order to avoid accusations of gross self‐interest, Asia Energy should hold itself to a higher standard if they hope to prove the broader necessity of the Project.  This SEIA is good for outlining the Project, but not sufficient for approving a loan.
