---

### Q1.1 "Tell me about yourself."

**Interviewer intent:** Establish who you are, what you bring, and why you are in this room. First impression. They want a coherent narrative, not a recitation of your résumé.

**Relevant evidence:** Technical training portfolio, Floodlines as flagship project, background in environmental/international policy, branding statement ("Data Storyteller & Analyst — Bridging Data, People, and Purpose"), DC location.

**What a strong answer demonstrates:**
- A clear professional identity (not "I'm trying to break into...")
- A through-line connecting past to present (why the transition makes sense)
- One or two specific, concrete anchors (a project, a result, a capability)
- Forward orientation (what you're looking for in this role)

**Common failure modes:**
- Starting with birth (chronological life story)
- Over-explaining the transition instead of presenting the identity
- Being so broad that nothing sticks
- Apologizing for the transition

**Likely follow-ups:** "Why data analytics?" / "Tell me more about the Floodlines project." / "What are you looking for in your next role?"

### Q3.3 "How do you approach a new dataset or analytical problem?"

**Interviewer intent:** Process, judgment, intellectual rigor. Are you methodical or impulsive?

**Answer outline (use Floodlines as the primary example):**
1. Start with the question: what decision or insight does this need to support? (not: "what can I do with this data?")
2. Understand the data generating process before analyzing: what is collected, how, by whom, with what incentives?
3. EDA first, always: distributions, missingness, outliers, class balance
4. Formalize assumptions explicitly
5. Build in robustness checks (Floodlines: 20+ model specs, LOVO, weight perturbation)
6. Report the range of results honestly, including negative or uncertain findings (R² ≈ 0 in Floodlines)
7. Translate to the audience appropriate for the output

### Q3.4 "Explain [specific technique] to me."

*This is a wildcard. Common targets: Moran's I, SHAP values, F₂ score, composite indices, Spearman correlation.*

**Preparation strategy:** For each major technique used in your projects, be able to:
- Explain it in plain English (no jargon)
- Explain why you chose it over alternatives
- Describe one result from your work using it
- Describe one limitation of it

**Example: SHAP**
- SHAP assigns each feature a value representing its contribution to a specific prediction — not a global average, but a per-prediction explanation
- Used in Salifort for all 4 models: found satisfaction_level + tenure ≈ 83–84% of feature importance across tree models
- Limitation: SHAP values can be computationally expensive for large datasets; correlation between features can complicate interpretation

### Q4.3 "Walk me through the Salifort employee churn project."

**Interviewer intent:** ML modeling proficiency; metric selection judgment; ability to connect technical choices to business context.

**Answer outline:**
1. The problem: synthetic dataset of 14,999 employee records; predict who will leave; 17% attrition rate (imbalanced)
2. The metric decision: chose F₂ score because the cost of missing a true leaver is higher than a false alarm in most HR contexts — explain the logic
3. Model comparison: 4 models (LR → DT → RF → XGBoost); XGBoost won with ROC AUC 0.982, recall 0.940
4. SHAP findings: satisfaction + tenure dominate; bimodal attrition (burnout vs. disengaged); every employee with 7 projects left
5. Limitations: synthetic dataset; findings not generalizable to real organizations

**Likely follow-ups:** "Why not just use accuracy?" / "How would you use this model in practice?" / "What does SHAP tell you that feature importance doesn't?"

---

### Q4.4 "How would you defend the validity of your Floodlines composite index?"

**Interviewer intent:** Methodological rigor. Can you be challenged?

**Answer outline:**
1. Any composite index involves value choices — I didn't hide that; I tested it explicitly
2. 20+ model specifications tested: different variables, different weights, two normalization methods (rank-based and z-score)
3. LOVO (leave-one-variable-out): tested the effect of removing each variable
4. Weight perturbation: tested robustness to weight variation
5. Result: rank-vs-z-score correlation was 0.89–1.00 across specs, and Spearman r vs. funding was consistently ~0.10–0.30 — the weak correlation finding is robust to methodology
6. The limitation I would readily concede: the index represents a particular view of vulnerability. Different stakeholders might construct a different index — this is a feature of composite indices, not a bug I can eliminate. I can only test robustness and be transparent about choices.

---

### Q4.5 "Your Salifort project used a synthetic dataset. How confident are you in those results?"

**Interviewer intent:** Intellectual honesty. Do you oversell your work?

**Answer outline:**
1. Acknowledge directly: the dataset is synthetic/fictional. I can't generalize findings to real organizations.
2. What the project demonstrates that is real: the methodology, the metric selection, the model comparison process, the SHAP interpretation — these are real skills demonstrated on a structured problem
3. What I'd want in a real organizational context: actual HR data with feature validation against domain experts before interpreting any SHAP finding as actionable
4. This is explicitly stated in the project documentation — not something I'm walking back in an interview

## Category 7: Communication with Nontechnical Stakeholders

---

### Q7.1 "How do you explain your findings to someone who doesn't understand statistics?"

**Answer outline:**
1. Start with the question they care about, not the method
2. Use analogy and comparison rather than statistical language (e.g., "the system responds to past flood claims about 3 times more strongly than it responds to current community need")
3. Be explicit about what the numbers can and cannot tell us
4. Offer the technical detail as optional depth, not required context

**Example from Floodlines:** "I wouldn't start by saying 'the Spearman correlation is 0.10.' I'd say: 'For every $1 of federal flood mitigation funding in Vermont, about $0.00 of it can be predicted by how vulnerable a community is today. The system responds much more strongly to whether that town has flooded before and filed claims.'"

---

### Q7.2 "How do you decide what to put in a report vs. leave out?"

**Answer outline:**
1. Lead with the decision the audience needs to make, not the analysis I found interesting
2. Every piece of content must do one of three things: tell them what happened, explain why it matters, or help them decide what to do
3. Limitations belong in the report, not as buried footnotes, but calibrated to what the decision-maker actually needs to know to act responsibly

---

### Q7.3 "How do you handle it when a stakeholder doesn't like your findings?"

**Answer outline:**
1. The methodology and the finding are separate questions — I am open to challenging either
2. If the challenge is methodological (legitimate): engage honestly, test the alternative specification, show the comparison
3. If the challenge is "I don't like what this says": note the concern, re-examine for any errors, but do not change the finding to please someone
4. The Floodlines robustness testing (20+ specs) is an example of building a defense in advance — the finding wasn't changed regardless of which reasonable methodology was used

## Polished Sample Answers

*These are starting points, not scripts. Calibrate length to the room: some interviewers want 90 seconds; others want five minutes. Know the difference.*

### Sample Answer 1: "Tell me about yourself."

*(Target length: 2–3 minutes)*

I'm a data analyst and researcher based in Washington DC. My work sits at the intersection of data analysis and public policy — specifically the kind of questions where the data exists but hasn't been asked the right questions yet.

My most recent independent project, Floodlines, is a good example of how I work. I analyzed whether FEMA's Hazard Mitigation Assistance funding in Vermont actually aligns with flood risk and community vulnerability across the state's 250-plus municipalities. The short answer is: it doesn't. More than half of Vermont's municipalities received no federal flood mitigation funding at all, and the correlation between my modeled need index and actual funding allocation was weak — somewhere between 0.10 and 0.30 depending on methodology. The strongest predictor of whether a town received funding was whether that town had already filed insurance claims in the past — which tells you the system responds to history, not to forward-looking risk. That finding held across twenty-plus model specifications, two normalization methods, and several sensitivity tests.

### Sample Answer 3: "Walk me through your Floodlines project."

*(Target length: 4–6 minutes; adjust to interviewer pace)*

Floodlines started with a question that seemed simple: does FEMA's Hazard Mitigation Assistance program — the one that funds flood mitigation infrastructure and buyouts — actually reach Vermont's most flood-vulnerable communities?

The reason Vermont is an interesting test case is that it was devastated by Hurricane Irene in 2011 and again by significant flooding in 2023. There's been real federal money flowing into the state, but it hasn't been analyzed at the municipal level against a systematic measure of need.

The analytical challenge is that "need" is not something FEMA publishes. I had to construct it. I built a composite need index from several data sources: FEMA's National Risk Index for baseline flood risk, the National Flood Insurance Program's claims history for actual insured loss experience, and Census ACS data for social vulnerability — poverty rates, proportion of elderly residents, households without vehicle access. Then I inflation-adjusted all the HMA funding figures to 2025 dollars so I was comparing across years fairly.

The core of the methodology was testing robustness. Any composite index involves judgment calls — what variables to include, how to weight them, how to normalize them. Rather than commit to one specification and defend it, I tested over twenty model specifications across two normalization methods (rank-based and z-score), performed leave-one-variable-out sensitivity analysis, and varied the weights systematically. The rank-vs-z-score correlation across those specifications was 0.89 to 1.00, which tells me the findings are not sensitive to my specific methodological choices.

The findings: more than 52% of Vermont's municipalities received no FEMA HMA funding at all. The Spearman correlation between my need index and actual funding allocation was roughly 0.10 to 0.30 across model specs — a weak relationship. The correlation between past NFIP insurance claims and funding was about 0.55 — substantially stronger. That's the key finding: the system responds to historical claims records, not to forward-looking modeled need.

I also ran a logistic regression to predict whether a town received any funding at all — AUC around 0.70, which is modest but interpretable. And an OLS regression to predict funding amounts — R-squared approximately zero, which means the model can't explain variation in how much money towns receive at all. I ran spatial autocorrelation using Moran's I, which showed that my need index has significant geographic clustering, but funding does not. The geographic distribution of funding doesn't follow the geographic distribution of risk.

The output is a public interactive dashboard on GitHub Pages — choropleth maps, a quadrant map that plots need vs. funding, scatter plots, a model switcher that lets you toggle between specifications, a town detail panel, and a narrative guided tour for visitors who aren't familiar with how to read the charts. I also published a technical appendix with full methodology and a separate narrative article.

What I think is most important about this project isn't the specific finding — it's that the finding is honest. I didn't tune the index to produce a dramatic result. The weak correlation is the result, and I documented it across 20-plus specifications precisely so that someone couldn't reasonably argue it's an artifact of my choices.

### Sample Answer 4: "Walk me through the Capital Crashpad project."

*(Target length: 3–4 minutes)*

Capital Crashpad is a longitudinal analysis of Washington DC's short-term rental market. I've been collecting quarterly snapshots from Inside Airbnb since Q2 2023, and I now have eleven snapshots covering Q2 2023 through Q2 2026.

The central question came into focus around Q2 2024, when I noticed a dramatic drop in the listing data. From roughly 6,700 listings to roughly 4,900 — about 1,800 listings gone in a single quarter, a 26% decline. That coincided with Airbnb's widely reported platform-wide removal of non-compliant listings, during which they reportedly removed over 200,000 listings nationally. The timing matched.

What made this analytically interesting rather than just a market observation is the regulatory angle. DC has a 30-night threshold for short-term rental licensing — listings with minimum stays of 31 or more nights are categorized differently under DC's STR ordinance. When I looked at the breakdown of the 1,800-listing decline, approximately 1,200 of those were 31-plus night minimum-stay listings. That's a very specific and interpretable pattern: the listings that disappeared were disproportionately the ones operating just above DC's licensing threshold.

The other findings: the share of licensed listings increased post-contraction, which suggests the enforcement did push the market toward compliance. Revenue per listing declined — most sharply for the 31-plus night category. But revenue concentration among top hosts was unchanged — the market shrank, it became more licensed, but it did not democratize. The same cluster of high-volume hosts still captures a disproportionate share of revenue. And the geographic distribution of the decline was broad — nearly every DC neighborhood was affected, not just tourist corridors.

A few things I'm explicit about in the documentation: the revenue figure is an upper-bound proxy calculated as price times occupied days — it's not actual reported revenue, and I don't present it as such. The "likely commercial" classification for high-listing-count hosts is a heuristic. And this is an observational study — I can describe the correlation between Airbnb's enforcement announcement and the listing drop, but I can't establish causation from the data alone.

What I like about this project is that it's genuinely ongoing. I'm not analyzing a historical dataset — I'm maintaining a living panel. The project will look different in two years than it does today, and I've built the infrastructure to keep adding to it.
