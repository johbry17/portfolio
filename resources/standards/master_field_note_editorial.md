I want to bring this project to completion. This is a comprehensive review, reconciliation, and editorial rewrite—not a light copyedit.

Your task is to understand the project in its entirety, establish what the evidence actually supports, resolve inconsistencies, and produce polished, publication-ready final versions of all five deliverables:

1. **Overview**
2. **Analysis**
3. **Data**
4. **Methods**
5. **GitHub README**

The finished project should be analytically sound, internally consistent, clearly documented, reproducible, and ready for public presentation in my portfolio.

## 1. First: Understand the entire project

Before editing prose, inspect the repository thoroughly.

Examine:
- Project structure and configuration files.
- All source code, scripts, notebooks, and analytical workflows.
- Raw, intermediate, and processed data, where accessible.
- Data cleaning, transformation, and validation procedures.
- Generated charts, tables, and analytical outputs.
- Existing Overview, Analysis, Data, and Methods pages.
- Existing README and other relevant documentation.
- Source references, external links, citations, and figure references.

Trace important findings back to the code and underlying data wherever possible.

Do not assume that existing prose, charts, or code comments are correct simply because they are already present. Determine what the project actually does and what its results demonstrate.

## 2. Audit and reconcile before rewriting

Conduct a systematic review of the entire project.

Look for:
- Conflicting figures, dates, counts, percentages, units, and sample sizes.
- Differences between written claims and actual analytical results.
- Inconsistent definitions or terminology across pages.
- Missing, incomplete, or incorrectly described data sources.
- Unsupported interpretations or claims that overstate the evidence.
- Methodological decisions that are undocumented or insufficiently explained.
- Missing caveats, data-quality problems, and coverage limitations.
- Broken or incorrect links, citations, figure references, and navigation.
- Redundant or misplaced material across the four pages.

Create an internal discrepancy and issue log. For each material issue, identify the affected pages, the underlying evidence, and the proposed resolution.

**Resolve discrepancies using the underlying code, data, and authoritative sources—not by choosing whichever number appears most often in the existing prose.**

Do not invent results, silently discard inconvenient evidence, or manufacture certainty.

If an issue cannot be resolved from available evidence, retain the appropriate qualification and document the unresolved issue. Ask me only when a decision genuinely requires my input.

Do not make unnecessary changes to sound analytical code simply to accommodate a preferred narrative. If code or analysis needs correction, explain why and make the correction only when justified.

## 3. Apply the Field Notes editorial standard

This project belongs to a series of data-driven studies. All projects share a four-page architecture and consistent editorial conventions, while retaining their own distinctive subject, narrative, structure, and personality.

### Overview — The story

Write an accessible, engaging, standalone account of the project.

It should:
- Establish the subject and central research question.
- Explain why the question matters.
- Introduce the relevant context without overwhelming the reader.
- Present the most important findings in a coherent narrative.
- Interpret what the evidence means.
- Explain what the evidence cannot establish.
- Leave the reader with a clear understanding of the project and its significance.

Write for an intelligent general audience, not exclusively for analysts or subject-matter specialists.

The Overview is not an abstract, executive summary, or condensed technical report. It is the publication's principal story.

### Analysis — The evidence

Present the analytical work supporting the Overview.

Include relevant visualizations, tables, statistical results, comparisons, diagnostics, and supplementary findings.

Organize the material into a coherent analytical progression. Introduce sections and explain what the evidence shows. Do not simply stack charts, tables, and model outputs.

The Analysis page should provide sufficient detail for an interested reader to understand and evaluate the findings without duplicating the Overview.

### Data — The inventory

Document the data used in the project.

For each source or dataset, explain:
- Source and provider, with links.
- Dataset or series name.
- Variables and definitions.
- Units and frequency.
- Geographic and temporal coverage.
- Relevant transformations or derived fields, where appropriate.
- Data-quality issues, gaps, limitations, and known anomalies.

Make clear what the data represents and what it does not.

### Methods — The research notebook

Explain how and why the analysis was conducted.

Document:
- Research design and analytical scope.
- Inclusion and exclusion criteria.
- Data acquisition, cleaning, reconciliation, and processing.
- Calculations, indicators, and derived measures.
- Classification and aggregation decisions.
- Statistical techniques and analytical assumptions.
- Sensitivity checks and robustness assessments, where applicable.
- Limitations and implications for interpretation.
- Reproducibility and execution instructions.

The distinction is important:

**Data explains what the numbers are and where they came from. Methods explains what I did with them and why.**

### GitHub README — The project entry point

Write a concise, polished README that introduces the project to someone encountering the repository for the first time.

It should:
- Explain the project and its central question.
- Summarize the principal findings or purpose.
- Describe the repository structure.
- Identify the primary data sources.
- Explain the analytical tools and technologies used.
- Provide appropriate setup and reproduction instructions.
- Link to the published project and its four pages, where applicable.
- Include relevant acknowledgments, data-source attribution, and licensing information where established.

The README is not a fifth analytical chapter. It is the project's front door for visitors, prospective employers, collaborators, and other analysts.

## 4. Editorial voice and style

My preferred voice is:

**Intellectually serious, conversationally accessible, lightly irreverent, and unwilling to pretend the data says more than it does.**

Be technically precise first. Then, where appropriate, translate a complicated finding into plain language or use a memorable phrase, dry quip, or quick metaphor.

I want the writing to sound like a thoughtful, curious human who understands both the subject and the analysis—not an academic paper, corporate report, or generic AI-generated article.

Specific preferences:
- Clear, direct prose.
- Active voice.
- Varied sentence structure and natural rhythm.
- Accessible explanations of technical concepts.
- Short paragraphs where appropriate.
- Memorable language when it genuinely improves understanding.
- No forced humor, gimmicks, or formulaic personality.
- No unnecessary jargon or inflated academic language.
- No repetitive caveats or boilerplate conclusions.

Distinguish clearly between:
- What the data demonstrates.
- What the findings suggest.
- What is a plausible interpretation.
- What remains speculative.
- What cannot be established.

Do not confuse correlation with causation or descriptive evidence with causal conclusions.

Preserve my voice and the project's distinctive personality. Do not make every project sound identical.

## 5. Series-wide presentation conventions

Maintain consistency in:
- Page titles, navigation, and section hierarchy.
- Typography and heading levels.
- Chart and table styling.
- Number, date, currency, and percentage formatting.
- Figure numbering, captions, and source notes.
- Citations and external links.
- Terminology and definitions.
- Cross-page references.

Chart captions should explain what matters about a figure, not merely repeat its title or describe its axes.

Use consistent conventions without forcing identical section sequences, chart counts, narrative structures, or conclusions across projects.
