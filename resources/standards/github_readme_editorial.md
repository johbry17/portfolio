## Editorial Standard: Field Notes / Studies — GitHub README

Treat each README as the **introductory and technical gateway** to a published Field Note—not as a fifth research document or a duplicate of the Overview.

**Purpose and audience:** Orient technically minded readers to the project: what it investigates, what it found, what evidence supports the findings, what limitations matter, and how to explore or reproduce the work. The general public-facing narrative belongs primarily in the Overview.

**Standard content:** Include, as appropriate:
- Project title, subtitle, and links to the published Field Note's four pages: Overview, Analysis, Data, and Methods.
- A concise project overview: research question, significance, scope, and approach.
- Key findings, accurately summarized and appropriately qualified.
- Research design: unit of analysis, sample, indicators, outcomes, and analytical approach.
- Principal data sources, with links to original providers.
- A selection of the most informative figures (typically 3–6, not a quota).
- Tools and technologies.
- Pipeline and reproducibility instructions, including dependencies, execution order, and rendering.
- A concise repository structure.
- Important data-quality issues and research limitations.
- References, licensing, and author attribution.

**Editorial approach:** Be clear, technically precise, and concise. Lead with the research, not the technology stack. Explain important distinctions among sources, indicators, samples, and measures. Make uncertainty, missing data, data-quality problems, and methodological limitations visible. Distinguish observed findings from interpretation, speculation, and causal claims.

**Relationship to the four research pages:** The README summarizes and links; it does not reproduce. Detailed results and interpretation belong in Analysis; full source and variable documentation in Data; and detailed research design, decisions, assumptions, and reproducibility in Methods.

**Consistency:** Keep the README aligned with all four published pages and the actual repository. Verify that titles, dates, sample sizes, units, definitions, findings, figures, limitations, instructions, and links agree. Document the actual pipeline rather than an idealized one.

**Finished vs. work in progress:** Development READMEs may include provisional findings, progress, outstanding tasks, and planned work. Finished READMEs should describe the completed project, remove obsolete development notes, and clearly identify any remaining limitations or future work.

**Preserve individuality:** These are shared editorial conventions, not a rigid template. Adapt headings, section order, length, figures, and technical depth to the project. Include only sections that add value; do not force a table of contents, infographic, fixed figure count, or unnecessary technical detail.

**Licensing:** Clearly distinguish original code, original writing and figures, and third-party data or assets. A suggested default is MIT for original code and CC BY 4.0 for original research content, subject to the terms governing third-party materials.

**When reviewing or editing:** Prioritize analytical integrity, reproducibility, and consistency with the published research before prose polish. Preserve the project's distinctive character. Never make the README sound more certain, comprehensive, or reproducible than the underlying work supports.
