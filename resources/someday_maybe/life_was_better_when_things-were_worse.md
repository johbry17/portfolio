# Field Note Idea: Life Was Better Back When Things Were Worse

**Working title:** *Life Was Better Back When Things Were Worse*
**Series:** Field Notes / Studies
**Status:** Someday / Maybe
**Proposed comparison:** 1996 vs. 2026 (or 1995 vs. 2025, depending on data availability)

## The central question

Was life actually better for the average American 30 years ago, or is nostalgia distorting our perception of the past?

Americans frequently express the belief that life was simpler, more affordable, safer, or generally better in decades past. Yet the 1990s were, by many measures, a time of lower material prosperity, shorter life expectancy, and less educational attainment.

Have ordinary Americans actually become better off? Or have some aspects of life deteriorated even as others improved?

The goal is to investigate the tension between **what we remember, what we believe, and what the evidence measures.**

## Research questions

1. **Material prosperity:** Does the typical American have greater purchasing power today? How have real median household income, earnings, housing affordability, and essential expenses changed?
2. **Health and longevity:** Are Americans living longer and healthier lives? How have life expectancy, infant mortality, healthcare access, and major causes of death changed?
3. **Education and opportunity:** Has educational attainment increased? Has education translated into better economic opportunities and social mobility?
4. **Everyday life:** Have safety, leisure time, working conditions, and quality of life improved?
5. **Financial security:** How has the cost of being an ordinary person changed? Consider housing, healthcare, childcare, higher education, and the ability to accumulate wealth.
6. **Geographic inequality:** Have improvements been shared equally across the country? Which states and regions have gained or lost ground?

## Potential indicators

| Dimension | Candidate measures |
|---|---|
| Income | Real median household income; median individual earnings |
| Affordability | Housing costs relative to income; rent burden; homeownership |
| Health | Life expectancy; infant mortality; healthcare coverage |
| Education | High school and college completion; educational attainment |
| Safety | Violent crime; traffic fatalities |
| Well-being | Self-reported happiness; life satisfaction; social trust |
| Opportunity | Earnings by education; intergenerational mobility |
| Financial security | Household wealth; debt; essential expenses |

Prioritize indicators with consistent historical definitions and comparable data.

## The geographic angle

An important extension is examining whether national improvements conceal substantial regional differences.

Potential comparisons:
- Expensive, opportunity-rich metropolitan areas (DC, Boston, New York, San Francisco).
- Rural and smaller communities.
- Fast-growing Sun Belt regions.
- Economically distressed areas, particularly communities affected by industrial decline.

Potential analytical centerpiece: Compare changes in real median household income against changes in housing affordability across states. Identify places where incomes increased but housing became less affordable, and places where both improved.

## Analytical approach

- Compare 1996 and 2026 where data permit, using 1995/2025 or 1996/2024 as alternatives when necessary.
- Prefer medians over averages when measuring typical economic conditions.
- Adjust monetary values for inflation.
- Distinguish households, individuals, workers, and the overall population.
- Examine national trends alongside selected state, regional, and demographic breakdowns.
- Show absolute and percentage changes.
- Avoid collapsing diverse quality-of-life indicators into a single composite score.
- Distinguish period comparisons (Americans then vs. Americans now) from life-course comparisons (different generations at comparable ages).

## Potential data sources

- [U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/) — income, education, housing, demographics.
- [FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/) — historical economic indicators.
- [Bureau of Economic Analysis](https://www.bea.gov/) — income and regional economic data.
- [National Center for Health Statistics](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/) — longevity, mortality, health.
- [Bureau of Justice Statistics](https://bjs.ojp.gov/) — crime and victimization.
- [Federal Housing Finance Agency](https://www.fhfa.gov/data/hpi) — housing price indices.
- [General Social Survey](https://gss.norc.org/) — happiness, attitudes, social trust.
- [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/) — historical social and economic analysis.
- [World Values Survey](https://www.worldvaluessurvey.org/) — attitudes and well-being.

## Potential narrative

1. **The nostalgia problem:** Introduce the familiar belief that the past was better. What does that actually mean?
2. **The scorecard:** Compare six to eight indicators across the two periods.
3. **The contradictions:** Identify areas of genuine progress alongside stagnation or deterioration.
4. **The geography:** Explore whether national improvements were broadly shared or concentrated in particular regions and populations.
5. **The conclusion:** What have Americans gained, what have they lost, and what does the evidence actually tell us about whether life is better?

## Editorial considerations

The political charge is a feature, not a problem, provided the research remains evidence-driven and politically neutral.

Avoid:
- Predetermining that life is better or worse today.
- Treating economic growth as synonymous with improved quality of life.
- Treating nostalgia as necessarily irrational.
- Confusing national averages with the experiences of typical households.
- Ignoring distributional differences.
- Making causal claims from simple before-and-after comparisons.

An important distinction: material living standards and subjective life satisfaction are related but different. Improvements in measurable conditions do not automatically mean people feel happier or more secure.

## Portfolio potential

A particularly strong demonstration of data storytelling and analytical versatility:

- Historical data acquisition and harmonization.
- Inflation adjustment and time-series analysis.
- Demographic and geographic comparisons.
- State-level choropleths and small-multiple visualizations.
- Distributional analysis.
- Clear communication of competing indicators and nuanced findings.

**Initial scope:** A compact national comparison using six to eight indicators, with a geographic analysis as the major extension.

**Guiding principle:** Let the data investigate the contradiction in the title rather than forcing the data to resolve it.

**The underlying question:** Have we actually made life better, or have we simply made some things better while making other things harder?
