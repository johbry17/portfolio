## 1. The City Apart: South Africa's geography of inequality  

Strongest overall candidate

The question: How much does the spatial legacy of apartheid still determine people's access to opportunity?

This is a fascinating intersection of urban planning, economics, history, transportation, and social geography.

You could investigate:
- Where people live relative to employment centers.
- How income and wealth vary geographically.
- Access to public transportation and essential services.
- Differences between historically segregated neighborhoods.
- Whether urban growth is reducing or reproducing spatial inequality.

Potential methods: GIS, spatial joins, census data, demographic analysis, accessibility mapping.

There's an especially interesting research lead here: a 2025 World Bank working paper examining spatial mismatches between residential populations and jobs in South African cities. It found substantial gaps in job accessibility, particularly for poorer neighborhoods. 
[World Bank](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099909301082687725/pdf/IDU-73635a73-71a1-4ea2-89d0-51278c8bf8fd.pdf)


The Gauteng City-Region Observatory (GCRO) has an unusually rich data portal, including:
- Census and ward-level demographics.
- Quality of Life surveys.
- Public infrastructure.
- Urban footprint and land-use data.
- Biodiversity layers.
[GCRO Open Data Portal](https://www.gcro.ac.za/outputs/dataportal/).

What you'd demonstrate: Spatial data analysis, GIS, urban research, economic geography, and the ability to turn a complicated historical issue into an understandable visual story.

This is a particularly good opportunity to develop your spatial-analysis capabilities beyond making maps.

## 2. The Green Paradox: Who gets to benefit from conservation?

The question: Can biodiversity conservation create economic opportunities for the communities living alongside protected ecosystems?

South Africa offers a particularly interesting setting because conservation isn't simply a matter of establishing national parks.

There's a complicated relationship between:
- Private land ownership.
- Community land rights.
- Protected areas.
- Wildlife tourism.
- Rural livelihoods.
- Biodiversity conservation.
One avenue is South Africa's Biodiversity Stewardship Programme, which uses agreements with private and communal landowners to expand conservation beyond formally declared protected areas.

A recent World Bank assessment examines 20 years of this program. 
[World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/en/brief/2026/09/08/analysis-of-20-years-of-implementing-south-africa-s-biodiversity-stewardship-progr)


You could investigate:
- Where biodiversity priority areas overlap with different land-tenure arrangements.
- The geography of protected areas versus areas of high biodiversity importance.
- The potential role of community-based conservation.
- Whether conservation opportunities are geographically aligned with rural development needs.

What you'd demonstrate: Environmental policy, conservation economics, spatial analysis, land-use data, and international development.

This also opens up a subject you haven't really explored in your existing projects: the economics of natural capital.

One caution: don't try to claim that conservation produces better livelihoods without actual socioeconomic outcome data. The geographic overlap itself is a worthwhile investigation.

## 3. The Electricity Divide: Who powers South Africa?

The question: What does the transition from coal to renewable energy mean for a country with profound economic inequality?

South Africa is an interesting energy case:
- Historically dependent on coal.
- Significant renewable energy potential.
- Electricity infrastructure and reliability challenges.
- Unequal household access and affordability.
- A transition with enormous implications for employment and regional economies.
Possible lines of investigation:
- Electricity access versus electricity reliability.
- Renewable energy investment by province.
- The geographic concentration of coal employment and infrastructure.
- The relationship between energy poverty and household income.
- The geography of renewable energy potential versus existing energy infrastructure.

What you'd demonstrate: Energy economics, environmental policy, infrastructure analysis, and spatial visualization.

This could also be an opportunity to work with electricity-generation and emissions datasets rather than the socioeconomic indicators you've used extensively.

## 4. The Water Question: Living in a water-stressed country

The question: How do water availability, infrastructure, and population growth intersect in South Africa?

Cape Town's 2017–2018 water crisis is the obvious entry point, but I'd actually be interested in going beyond it.

Potential investigations:
- Water availability across provinces and municipalities.
- Water infrastructure and service delivery.
- Urban population growth versus water resources.
- Water quality and wastewater treatment.
- The relationship between water stress and socioeconomic inequality.

There's a good national data resource in the Water Research Observatory, maintained by South Africa's Water Research Commission.

[Water Research Observatory](https://www.waterresearchobservatory.org/).

[Stats SA](https://www.statssa.gov.za/) also publishes SDG indicators covering wastewater treatment, water quality, water stress, and access. 


What you'd demonstrate: Environmental data analysis, infrastructure policy, resource management, and potentially time-series and geospatial methods.

This one has an especially nice connection to your academic background in natural resource management, without repeating the tourism theme.


## 5. The Land Question: Who owns South Africa?

The question: How does the historical distribution of land ownership continue to shape economic opportunity?

This is probably the most politically and historically interesting possibility.

You could explore:
- The distribution of agricultural land ownership.
- Land reform and redistribution.
- Rural poverty and agricultural employment.
- Differences between commercial, communal, and state land.
- The relationship between land access and economic development.

South Africa's 2017 Land Audit provides a starting point, including ownership patterns by race and gender, although its coverage and definitions have limitations. 
[South African Government](https://www.gov.za/issues/land-reform)


What you'd demonstrate: Historical research, political economy, development economics, data auditing, and possibly GIS.

I particularly like this because it forces you to grapple with an important methodological question: what can administrative data actually tell us about a historically contested social issue?

6. The urban quality-of-life investigation

The question: What makes a city livable, and how does that differ across neighborhoods?

This is perhaps the most open-ended option.

The GCRO's Quality of Life survey is a remarkable resource. It has seven waves, from 2009 through 2023–24, covering households and individuals across Gauteng.

It includes:
- Housing and tenure.
- Water, sanitation, and electricity.
- Transport and mobility.
- Employment and household finances.
- Safety and crime.
- Education and health.
- Public participation and governance.
- Subjective well-being.

The latest survey is available through the World Bank Microdata Library. 
[Quality of Life Survey 2023-2024](https://microdata.worldbank.org/catalog/6658/study-description)


You could investigate something like:

Does access to urban services translate into perceived quality of life?

Or:

What explains differences in subjective well-being across the Gauteng metropolitan region?

You could combine survey analysis with geographic data, perhaps even build a neighborhood-level livability index.

What you'd demonstrate: Survey research, statistical analysis, data storytelling, urban studies, and potentially multivariate analysis.

This is also a chance to move beyond the country-level indicators you've been using in your development projects.
