This project is, quite literally, close to home for me; I live in DC. I stumbled on a sharp drop in Airbnb listings while digging through the data, and it caught my attention. Some internet sleuthing revealed it coincided with changes in company policy, not local laws. Routine EDA turned into a deeper investigation: the data showed a structural break, with lucrative extended-stay rentals disappearing. I built this tool for anyone in DC to explore these shifts, whether you're curious about your block or the big picture.

Project Overview

Between Q1 and Q2 2024, Washington, D.C. lost nearly 1,800 active Airbnb listings in a single quarter. Listings requiring minimum stays of 31 nights accounted for roughly 1,200 of that decline, while licensed listings made up a larger share of the remaining market. Capital Crashpad investigates this abrupt shift alongside Airbnb’s expanded identity verification and quality-removal efforts, examining how platform governance can reshape a local market beyond the reach of municipal law.

Using quarterly listing data from Inside Airbnb, I built a data pipeline with PostgreSQL and Python to examine changes in supply, licensing, minimum-night requirements, pricing, projected revenue, and host concentration. An interactive dashboard built with JavaScript and Leaflet lets users explore neighborhood patterns, while a guided case study connects those patterns to the city's regulatory context. The analysis also found that revenue remained highly concentrated among a small share of hosts, even after the market contracted.

The timing and scale of the decline are consistent with a major structural break, but the available data cannot prove that Airbnb’s policy changes caused it or establish why particular listings disappeared. The project distinguishes observed market changes from plausible explanations, including the limits of projected revenue and reviews as proxies for actual earnings and demand.

Capital Crashpad combines database querying, exploratory analysis, geospatial visualization, and evidence-based writing to make a complex local market shift accessible to residents and policymakers. It demonstrates how I approach data storytelling: identifying a consequential pattern, investigating its context, and building tools that help people explore the evidence for themselves.

Data Storytelling

Capital Crashpad includes a guided, interactive data story that leads viewers through the market's contraction, changing licensing patterns, persistent host concentration, and differences across DC neighborhoods, before handing the dashboard back to the viewer to explore.
