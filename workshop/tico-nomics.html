Capital, Policy, and Progress: The World Bank and Costa Rica

How has the World Bank Group's relationship with Costa Rica changed over 45 years? A look at shifting lending priorities, financial instruments, and development ambitions—from structural adjustment to climate-linked reform.

Costa Rica is often held up as a development success story: a country that invested in education, health, and environmental protection while building a relatively prosperous economy. I studied in Costa Rica while earning my master's in Natural Resources and Sustainable Development; I've glimpsed the nuance behind that success story.

For this project, I just wanted to play with World Bank data. It's as good a reason as any.

Project Overview

Over four and a half decades, Costa Rica's relationship with the World Bank Group shifted from infrastructure investment and structural adjustment toward social development, private-sector investment, and climate policy. What can the institution's financing reveal about those changing priorities?

I examined World Bank Group activity in Costa Rica from 1980 to 2025, bringing together project and financial records from its three main institutions: IBRD, IFC, and MIGA. Using R, I reconciled project records, adjusted lending figures for inflation, classified financing instruments and sectors, and analyzed the language of available project descriptions.

The results show a striking shift in scale. Twelve IBRD projects approved between 2020 and 2025 account for nearly 58% of the period's total nominal commitments. Recent lending has also increasingly linked fiscal reform with decarbonization and climate objectives.

This is a history of institutional priorities, not a measure of development impact. But tracing the money—and the language used to justify it—offers a useful window into how development finance has changed alongside Costa Rica.
