Walk to Mordor

Real footsteps. Fictional journeys.

Three Journeys. One Walker. Two Clocks. An interactive data story mapping 5,196 miles of real-world walking onto three journeys through Middle-earth. Explore the same routes through two independent timelines: when I actually walked, and when Tolkien's characters did.

About the Project

GitHub Repository

I grew up reading Tolkien. Certified hobbit nerd; can quote The Silmarillion; pretty sure my mom read it while I was in utero. I first walked to Mordor during the pandemic, drawn to the books' ideas about perseverance, uncertainty, and finding beauty along the way. Gamifying a second walk with The Conqueror and earning shiny medals was a no-brainer. Three journeys, two years, and the length of the Silk Road later, I had a pile of walking data and some questions. How much did I walk? When did my pace change? How did the journeys compare?

I'm also a data nerd. Of course I was gonna build something.

Project Overview

Between March 2024 and June 2026, I completed three virtual walking challenges for Frodo, Aragorn, and Bilbo through The Conqueror, using daily distances recorded by Google Fit. Across the full two-year window, including gap periods between challenges, I walked 5,196 miles over 806 days.

The challenge was to bring two different timelines together on the same map. Walking records provided daily distances and dates; The Conqueror supplied milestone distances; Tolkien's books and The Atlas of Middle-earth provided the geographic and narrative framework. I created a custom route-drawing tool to establish the map geometry and associate individual route segments with challenge milestones, locations, and story events.

I then reconstructed a continuous Middle-earth timeline from Tolkien's chronology. Tolkien didn't write "October 19, 6.7 miles"; I needed to approximate that. I built a Shire Calendar, assigned fictional dates to known mileage markers, and interpolated the distances between them to create a daily record. The result is two parallel timelines, My Time and Middle-earth Time, that share the same hand-drawn route geometry while preserving their distinct dates, movement patterns, and narrative events.

The two timelines reveal different patterns of movement. On Middle-earth's compressed calendar, Aragorn covers the challenge distance at roughly 7.5 times my real-world walking pace. In my defense, he had a horse. Middle-earth Time also exposes long periods of fictional rest and captivity, while My Time tells a quieter story: the walking habit persisted even when no challenge was underway.

Technically, the project combines exploratory data analysis, temporal modeling, custom geospatial route rendering, and interactive visualization. More broadly, it reflects an approach I value in analytical work: making complex systems easier to understand and interrogate by making their underlying relationships visible. Here, that means more than plotting miles on a map; it means building a coherent system through which people can explore how time, geography, and narrative shape our understanding of a journey.
